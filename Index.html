23.1 Uses of statistics

What is statistics for?

Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing and interpreting data. To find out about a group of people or objects, the most common method is a statistical survey.

Example

Launching a new laptop

A company collects data on what makes customers choose a laptop (price, weight, battery life…) before deciding how to promote its new model.

Example

Fewer visitors at a theme park

The park studies customers' opinions about its facilities and service to find out why visitor numbers are falling.

23.1 Questionnaire design

Writing questions you can trust

A survey usually starts with a questionnaire written for a clear objective. Every question must be reliable and valid.

Reliability

Are the replies consistent?

Two people in the same situation should give the same answer. Subjective words such as a lot, a large amount or often mean different things to different people, so they make a question unreliable.

Validity

Does it measure the objective?

The question must ask about exactly what the objective needs, and its options must cover every possible case. Otherwise it is not valid.

23.1 Sampling

Population, census and sample

Population

The whole group of people or objects we want to study.

Census

A survey that collects data from all units in the population.

Sample

The units chosen from the population. The way we choose them is the sampling method.

Sample survey

A survey that collects data from some units only. If the sample is chosen carefully, its results help us make inferences about the population.

Why don't we always take a census?

Reason 1

The population is huge

Collecting every piece of data takes too much time and money, for example when studying the population density of the whole world.

Reason 2

Testing destroys the unit

To check canned food you must open the can. An opened can cannot be sold, so testing every can leaves nothing to sell.

Reason 3

Some units can't be reached

For example, people with drug addiction often hide it, so we cannot find all of them.

23.1 Sampling methods

How should we choose the sample?

Type I

Non-probability sampling

Not every unit in the population has a known chance of being selected.

Easy and cheap, and useful for finding people with special characteristics. But it depends on the researcher's judgement, so its results cannot be evaluated objectively.

Type II

Probability sampling

Every unit in the population has a known and non-zero chance of being selected.

Needed whenever we want to evaluate a sample objectively.

Three non-probability methods

Convenience sampling

Choose units that are easy to reach.

To study bus waiting times, interview passengers at the nearest bus stop.

Judgemental sampling

The researcher chooses units based on their own judgement.

To study the impact of COVID-19, a researcher judges tourism to be badly hit and interviews some airlines.

Snowball sampling

People already interviewed refer more people.

To study discrimination against South Asians, each interviewee introduces more South Asians to interview.

The names of these sampling methods are not required in the senior secondary curriculum, but you should know whether a method is probability or non-probability sampling.

    23.2 Abuses of statistics

    When statistics mislead

    If any stage of a survey is badly designed, the result can be an abuse of statistics: a wrong or misleading conclusion. Abuses happen in two places: sampling and data collection, and data analysis.

    A · Abuses in sampling and data collection

    I. Inappropriate sampling: the sample does not represent the population, so the results are biased.

    Sample from a specific area

    To study Internet use among the city's elderly, a team chooses elderly people from a computer class at a community centre.

    People in a computer class are more likely to use the Internet often, so the result is biased.

    Self-selection

    An online poll about political views is open to anyone who wants to vote.

    Mostly people with strong opinions choose to take part, so the sample is not representative.

    II. Inappropriate questionnaire design · Anson vs Jessica

    Objective: to find out whether Japanese drama or Korean drama was more popular among their classmates last weekend.

    Anson's questionnaire
    1. Did you watch Japanese drama last weekend?YesNo
    2. Did you watch Korean drama last weekend?YesNo

    Result: number of students who watched

    "Korean drama is more popular."

    Jessica's questionnaire
    1. How long did you watch Japanese drama last weekend? hours
    2. How long did you watch Korean drama last weekend? hours

    Result: total time spent watching

    "Japanese drama is more popular."

    Lab 1 · A higher percentage means more people?

    Last month, 10% of people in District A and 7% of people in District B caught a flu. Someone claims: "More people caught the flu in District A than in District B."

    Challenge: change the populations until the claim becomes false.

    The background information that is missing is the size of each population. A percentage alone does not tell you the number of people.

    Lab 2 · Averaging percentages

    Lab 3 · Percentage points vs percentage change

    A class's passing rate changes from test I to test II. Is the increase "10%"?

    The difference between two rates is measured in percentage points. The percentage change divides that difference by the original rate.

    Lab 4 · Drawing conclusions about causality too soon

    A study compares the time children spend playing computer games with the chance of being overweight. The researcher concludes: "Playing computer games causes children to be overweight."

    The graph shows that the two are related. Which theories could explain it? Tap each one.

    A graph shows a relationship, not a cause. Before concluding that one thing causes another, we need a theory that explains it and must rule out the other possible explanations.
